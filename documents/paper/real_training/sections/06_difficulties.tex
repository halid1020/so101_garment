\section{Difficulties}
\label{sec:difficulties}

This section records the obstacles met while building the platform and how
each was resolved, so the result is reproducible and its remaining weak
points are visible. Each obstacle gets one short subsection.

\paragraph{Temporal skew across streams.} Taking each stream's latest value
at frame time placed samples captured at different instants into one frame.
The resolution is to stamp every stream on read, align all streams to one
reference time per frame, and log the residual drift
(Section~\ref{sec:collection}). A residual remains: unsynchronised cameras
keep an irreducible cross-camera skew that we measure but cannot remove in
software, because their capture clocks are independent.

\paragraph{The stale-command labelling trap.} When no fresh command exists,
the recorded action falls back to the measured state. On a moving homing
frame this would teach a spurious command to hold still. The resolution is
the per-frame teleoperation flag, which marks such frames for exclusion,
together with a logged count of how often the fallback fired, so the
problem is visible per episode rather than hidden in the data.

\paragraph{Depth cannot use the colour pipeline.} Sixteen-bit depth pushed
through a three-channel colour-video encoder is quantised and corrupted.
The resolution is to store depth losslessly as a per-frame image beside the
dataset, keyed to the dataset frame index, with its scale and intrinsics
recorded alongside.

\paragraph{Sharing a peripheral bus.} This heading covers three separate
difficulties, and conflating them cost us a great deal of time, so we separate
them here. The first is bandwidth contention, which is genuine: several
uncompressed colour streams do not fit through shared controllers, and it is why
we require a compressed transport (Section~\ref{sec:collection}).

The second is a stream running below the rate it was asked for, which we had
attributed to the first and which we now know it was not. Measured alone and
then again with every other stream running, our wrist cameras delivered the same
rate; nothing was being starved of anything. A stream below its requested rate is
therefore not evidence of contention, and reading it as such sent us looking at
hubs and cables for a fault that lay in the camera's own exposure, which the next
paragraph takes up. We record the correction because the earlier account of this
platform made exactly this inference. \unjustified{} the number of simultaneous
streams a given host and hub can sustain remains machine-specific; we measure it
per rig rather than derive it.

Attaching the fingertip cameras took the suite past that ceiling for the first
time and showed us what exceeding it actually looks like, which is not what the
first difficulty above would lead one to expect. Contention here does not
degrade a stream; it refuses one. Past the ceiling a camera opens exactly as a
working camera does and then delivers nothing at all, indefinitely, while every
stream that was admitted keeps its full rate. The two failures therefore look
nothing alike, which is what makes the correction above safe to state: a stream
running slow is still not evidence of contention, because contention does not
produce slow streams. Two further properties decide how this must be handled.
Which stream is refused is arbitrary --- repeated runs of one configuration
denied different cameras --- so the symptom presents as an unreliable camera
rather than as a budget, and looking for the fault in the camera is the natural
and wrong response. Asking for less does not help either, and the reason is
structural rather than incidental: each of these cameras offers exactly one frame
rate for a given image format and size, so there is no slower mode to request.
Halving the frame size does move the fingertip cameras into their slower mode,
and even that admitted no additional stream, because the reservation follows what
a camera declares rather than what it sends.

The design consequence follows from the first property. A refusal that stays
silent is indistinguishable from a broken camera, so a stream that opens must be
required to prove it delivers before it is treated as open; a camera that opens
and yields nothing is a failed open, not a working one to be retried. Enforcing
that turns an unbounded retry loop --- which punishes the very bus the remaining
cameras are sharing --- into a question answered before recording starts, and
lets the operator be told which controller is over-subscribed and which streams
are competing on it. The host driver does offer a setting that makes it compute a
stream's true bandwidth need rather than trust the camera's declaration, and it
is the natural remedy to reach for; we tried it, reloading the driver so that
every device was enumerated under the new setting, and it admitted no additional
stream --- the fixup appears not to apply to the compressed transport this
platform requires. We report it because the ceiling is easier to accept once the
obvious escape from it has been measured and found not to help.

What remains is not a repair but a wiring decision, and one more measurement
settles how to make it. Capacity is not uniform across controllers, because the
views do not cost the same: one controller carried the overhead camera, a wrist
camera and one fingertip camera, while another could not carry a wrist camera and
two fingertip cameras. The fingertip views are the expensive ones. Since a hub
only fans out a controller's bus rather than adding to it, the remedy is to
spread the cameras over more controllers than the two the hubs occupy, in groups
the measurement above has shown to fit --- or, until then, to record a subset
that fits and say so.

The third is the one that actually costs a session: a hub failing as a unit and
taking every device behind it. Losing one stream is recoverable, because the
recorder holds the episode and resumes it when the stream returns
(Section~\ref{sec:collection}); losing a hub is not the same event repeated
several times over. The same hub that carries cameras and arms may also carry the
volume being written to, and then the fault does not interrupt the session --- it
removes what the session is being written into, mid-episode. Three measures
answer it, each described where it belongs: the readiness check reports which hub
every sensor and the storage depend upon, so the arrangement is known before a
session rather than discovered during one (Section~\ref{sec:replicability}); the
failures are grouped by hub when reported, because several devices failing at
once is one cause and not several, and blaming individual cables sends an
operator to reseat connectors that were never loose; and each episode is
committed as it is accepted, so a session interrupted this way keeps everything
it had already recorded.

\paragraph{Appearance drift, and the stronger reason beneath it.} Automatic
exposure and white balance make the same scene look different across episodes and
between collection and deployment, which is reason enough to fix them and to
record the values chosen, so that appearance is a controlled and documented
variable rather than a hidden one.

The stronger reason is not about appearance at all. Exposure bounds frame rate,
since no camera delivers frames faster than it exposes them, so an automatic
exposure that lengthens in dim light costs capture rate silently and reports
nothing (Section~\ref{sec:collection} quantifies the relation on our cameras).
This is the mechanism the preceding paragraph was mis-attributing to the bus, and
it is worth stating which way the evidence points: the appearance argument alone
had not persuaded us to fix exposure, whereas losing a third of the capture rate
did.

\paragraph{Episode boundaries inside a shared video file.} Episodes are not
stored one file each; several are packed end to end into one video, and an
episode is identified by the window it occupies inside that file. The end of
that window is exclusive --- one episode's end timestamp is exactly the next
one's start --- so the frame sitting on it belongs to the following recording.
A reader that treats the window as inclusive therefore ends every episode by
showing a frame of the next, which is subtle to notice because it looks like a
recording that merely ends abruptly. We record it because anyone implementing a
reader for this format meets it, and because the correct handling is not simply
to subtract one frame: independent decoders kept in step by seeking can stray
across such a seam by a frame, so a viewer is better off stopping fractionally
short of the end than trying to land exactly on it.

\paragraph{Host timing jitter.} Background processes and power-saving
governors cause the recorder's frame loop to overrun its budget. The
resolution is the readiness check's host-tuning items and the recorder's
own overrun warnings, which surface a slow tick immediately. \unjustified{}
the overrun and staleness thresholds are set by convention and have not
been tuned against downstream policy performance.

\paragraph{Coverage for autonomy.} Clean expert trajectories
under-represent the off-nominal and recovery states an autonomous policy
inevitably visits, where small errors compound~\citep{ross2011dagger}. The
resolution is a collection protocol that deliberately varies object
placement and includes recoveries from near-failures, in the spirit of the
low-cost bimanual demonstration collection of \citet{zhao2023aloha}. This is a data-design obligation on the
operator, not something the recording software can supply.
