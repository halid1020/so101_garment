\section{Replicability}
\label{sec:replicability}

Reproducing the platform needs its measured geometry, its camera
calibration, its rates, and its action constants. This section lists what
is measured and stored, and the procedure that confirms the rig is ready.

\paragraph{Geometry and provenance.} The rig geometry derives from one
parametric computer-aided-design description, and the same description
generates the digital twin. The documented dimensions and the physical
build therefore cannot drift apart. The validation object used for simple
pick-and-place checks is a printed cube of stated side length, sized so the
capped gripper can grasp it.

\paragraph{Camera calibration.} When a depth-capable overhead camera is
fitted, its intrinsic parameters and depth scale are read from the device and
stored with every dataset, so the depth images are metrically interpretable
after the fact. The exterior cameras are on fixed mounts, and their poses relative to
the rig are measured and recorded. A camera that shifts between collection
and deployment breaks a policy silently, so a fixed and recorded pose is
part of the platform, not an afterthought.

\paragraph{Rates and timing.} The dataset frame rate, the full-rate side
log rate, and the proprioception rate are stated for each session, together
with the measured drift budget of Section~\ref{sec:collection}. These
numbers let a reader judge the temporal quality of the data without
rerunning the rig.

\paragraph{Action constants.} The frozen action definition of
Section~\ref{sec:contract} --- units, joint order, gripper cap, input
scales, end-effector frame and orientation convention --- travels with the
dataset. It is exactly what an autonomous controller must reproduce, so
storing it beside the data removes a common source of silent train and
inference disagreement.

\paragraph{Readiness procedure.} A preflight check confirms, before
collection, that every arm is calibrated and bound to a stable alias, that
the wrist joints are limited to protect the fingertip cables, that the arms
are at their home pose, that every camera and the depth device open at
their configured resolution, that the dataset volume has headroom, and that
the host-tuning items which reduce timing jitter are set. It reports one
ready-or-not verdict, so an operator does not begin a long session on a rig
that is quietly misconfigured. The check is also read from the console of the
following paragraphs, immediately above the form that starts a session, so it
is consulted where the session begins rather than in a separate step that can
be skipped; and where the volume is the item that fails, that console is also
where the operator reclaims the space, so a failed check is answered in the
same place it is raised.

The check further reports which peripheral-bus hub each sensor and the
storage volume depend upon. This is not fussiness about cabling. A rig of
this kind is assembled from peripheral devices acquired at different times
and connected as sockets happen to be free, so unrelated components
accumulate behind one shared hub without anyone deciding that they should;
and a hub is a single point of failure for everything behind it, which
reports itself as several unrelated device errors rather than as one cause.
The two ways this can happen are not equally serious, and the check
separates them. Sensors sharing a hub costs an interruption the recorder can
already absorb, since a stream that disappears and returns only pauses the
episode. Storage sharing a hub with the very sensors recording into it is
different in kind: the same fault that stops the capture also removes the
file the capture is being written to, so the episode cannot be finished and
cannot be recovered. The operator is therefore given the grouping itself
rather than a bare warning, because the remedy is to choose a different
socket, and that requires knowing which devices presently share one.

A single guided pass calibrates all four
arms in turn, so bringing the rig to its calibrated state is one step
rather than four, and each arm's calibration is bound to its side rather
than to the order in which its bus happens to enumerate.

\paragraph{On-robot replay check.} A recorded episode can be played back on
the physical followers: the stored targets are re-sent frame by frame and
the measured joints are read back, so the difference between the recorded
and the reproduced motion is quantified per joint. This exposes any
open-loop tracking error --- gravity sag at reach, a stiff joint, a drifted
calibration --- that a purely on-screen review would hide, and it confirms
that the frozen action definition genuinely drives the arms as the dataset
claims. \unjustified{} the check is open-loop and single-arm-workspace, so
it measures follower tracking, not task success.

\paragraph{Episode review and curation.} A recorded dataset is inspected in
a browser-based console served on the collection host: it lists the
datasets, lists each dataset's recordings, and plays a selected recording as
the same composited sensor view the operator watched while collecting, with a
draggable scrub control to move through it. A flawed episode --- a mistimed
grasp, a dropped object, a stream that went stale --- can be removed there,
individually or in batches, and the dataset is rewritten with the remaining
episodes renumbered consistently across the training features and the side
files, so curation leaves a valid dataset rather than a gap. Reviewing and
pruning before training keeps a collection honest without re-running the rig.

\paragraph{Dataset curation.} Curation of whole datasets belongs in the same
place, because the mistakes it repairs are made in the sessions that produced
the recordings. The console therefore also names, renames, deletes and merges
the datasets on the collection volume. Naming is not a cosmetic concern here: a
collection started session by session from a shell accumulates near-duplicate
names, and a reviewer who cannot tell two of them apart cannot decide which to
train on, so every entry is listed with its frame rate, its recorded streams,
its instruction, its episode count and its size on the volume. Merging writes a
new dataset and leaves the sources as they are, so combining recordings is
never itself destructive; it is refused, with the reason stated before any work
begins, when the sources disagree on frame rate, robot type or recorded
streams, or when any source still holds episodes marked for removal, whose
indices would no longer denote the episodes the reviewer marked. Every
operation that replaces a dataset moves the original aside and swaps the result
into place only once it is complete, so an interruption leaves the collection
as it was rather than partly rewritten.

\paragraph{Binding devices to names.} A rig of this kind is assembled from
devices that are indistinguishable to software. The gripper and wrist cameras
are the same model bought together, and the arm buses present themselves in
whatever order they were connected, so which physical device answers to which
name is decided by the sockets they happen to occupy, and it changes whenever
something is replugged. The platform therefore keeps an explicit map from each
stream name to a socket-stable device alias, and identifies the devices for it
physically: a camera is shown live until the operator presses its gel and sees
which view changes, and an arm is recognised by wiggling it and watching which
port's raw joint counts move. The bus is opened uncalibrated with its torque
disabled for that test, so identification reads the arm and never drives it.

Two properties of this map matter more than the procedure. It is per machine
and never travels with the code, because it describes one rig's cabling.
And it is checked rather than trusted: the console shows, for every name, whether
the device it names is presently connected, since an assignment that has gone
stale otherwise announces itself only as a stream missing from a recording,
which is a poor place to discover it. Assignment is refused while a session is
recording, because the session holds those very devices.

\paragraph{Operating a session from the console.} Collection itself is driven
from the same page. One process must own the rig, because a camera opens once
and the arm buses are held under lock, so the console does not take the devices:
it starts the recorder as a session of its own and asks it for what the operator
needs to see. The session serves its frames and its state back over the
machine's loopback interface, and the console forwards them, so the live view
costs a single image compression per viewer and never touches the loop that
writes the dataset. Two consequences matter for replicability. A rig needs no
display of its own: the operator watches the streams, the recorder state, the
episode count against its target and any stream that has gone stale from
whatever machine they are sitting at. And the session outlives the page, so
closing or reloading the console cannot cost a recording.

What may be pressed remotely is deliberately narrow. Starting and ending an
episode, and ending the session, are decisions about the recording and are
available. Enabling, parking and homing the arms move them, and move them
before anyone has necessarily looked at the rig, so they remain on the headset
and on the keyboard beside it; the browser only reports whether the arms are
enabled, and refuses to start an episode until they are. Ending a session is a
request the recorder honours between frames, which is what parks the arms,
finishes an episode in progress and closes the dataset; the console escalates
only if that request is ignored, and never to a signal that would abandon an
open episode. \unjustified{} the division of controls is a judgement about
where an operator's eyes are, not a measured safety result.

\paragraph{Motion review.} Playing an episode back shows whether a
demonstration did the right thing. It does not show whether it did it
\emph{smoothly}, and a jerky demonstration is a defect a policy imitates.
Smoothness is a property of derivatives, and the dataset stores none: it stores
joint positions at a fixed rate. The review tool therefore differentiates them,
reporting each joint's velocity and acceleration and each arm's linear and
angular end-effector rates, both as the value under the playhead and as a plot
of the whole episode, so that a reviewer sees a single-frame snap instead of
having to catch it by eye.

Two things make that more than decoration. The first is that the derivative is
legible at the rate we record, which we measured rather than assumed. Our joint
encoders resolve about a tenth of a degree, which at the dataset rate leaves an
acceleration noise floor near \SI{20}{\degree\per\second\squared} beneath motion
that reaches some \SI{400}{\degree\per\second\squared} --- roughly an order of
magnitude of headroom. Plain central differences therefore suffice, and we apply
no smoothing at all: we tried a standard polynomial smoother across every
practical window and it enlarged the residual on stationary frames rather than
reducing it, because a fit spanning more samples draws in neighbouring motion.
The transients such a filter attenuates are precisely what this view exists to
show.

The second is that the end-effector motion is derived rather than read. The
Cartesian channels are recorded only in the pose-tracking teleoperation mode, so
every episode driven from the leader arms stores joints alone. The review tool
reconstructs the tool-frame trajectory through the platform's own kinematic
model, expressed in each arm's base frame, which is the convention the recorded
Cartesian channels follow where they exist, so a derived pose and a recorded one
denote the same quantity. Cartesian smoothness can therefore be judged on
recordings that never stored a Cartesian stream. \unjustified{} the noise floor
is measured against our encoders at our frame rate, and the acceleration at
which a reviewer ought to reject an episode rests on judgement, not on any
measurement of what a policy tolerates.
