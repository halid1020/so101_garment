# The framewise comparison — paragraph flow

The section the videos belong to. Its job is to make one point that no pooled
figure in this report can make: **a mean over a recording is the wrong summary
for a channel that matters for a second of it.** Everything else here supports
that or bounds it.

- P1 opens and forward-links: two moving pictures and two still figures, all
  from ONE recording no policy trained on, all showing the same instants.
- P2 how they were built, in plain English and naming no scripts: the same
  recording is replayed frame by frame; at each frame every policy plans, each
  input is suppressed in turn, and the change in the plan is recorded; the
  panels of one video frame are therefore the same moment for every policy.
  Say why that matters — separate videos played side by side drift apart, and a
  reader cannot then tell whether two panels differ because the POLICIES differ
  or because the MOMENTS do.
- P3 the alignment rule, as a rule and not a footnote: the video covers only
  frames every policy scored, so a policy that stopped early shortens the video
  rather than shifting the others.
- P4 the result, in bold: touch swings within one recording by far more than it
  differs between policies. Give the range against the pooled mean. This is the
  paragraph the whole section exists for.
- P5 the second result, in bold and genuinely new: the two families do not read
  touch the same WAY. One reads it in bursts, the other steadily. Peak against
  mean is the statistic; say which family is which and resist explaining why.
- P6 the Grad-CAM still: what a reader should look at, that a cropped arm's
  panel is drawn from the CROPPED image, and the resolution caveat between
  families — the maps differ in grid size by family, so compare within a column
  and not across one.
- P7 what these pictures cannot settle, forward to threats: attribution is a
  difference and not an importance, one recording is not a sample, and a bright
  map is not a correct plan.
