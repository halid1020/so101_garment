\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

Every model reads the joint positions of both arms (twelve numbers, which we
call \emph{proprioception}) and some of the cameras. Each outputs a
\emph{chunk}: a plan of future arm commands at \SI{30}{\hertz}, the rate at
which the demonstrations were recorded. \new{(We checked the camera rate in
the recordings; Appendix~\ref{sec:provenance}.)} Table~\ref{tab:models} lists
how the models differ, and Figure~\ref{fig:inputs} shows the images
each one receives. For each model we give its original design first and then what we
changed.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{model_inputs.png}
  \caption{\new{The images each model receives, at one moment of held-out
  demonstration 58. LLG, LRG, RLG and RRG are the fingertip sensors: left arm
  left gripper finger, left arm right finger, right arm left finger and right
  arm right finger.}}
  \label{fig:inputs}
\end{figure}

\textbf{ACT} \citep{zhao2023act} \new{encodes each camera image separately with
one shared ResNet-18 and joins the resulting tokens with the proprioception.}
A transformer, trained as a conditional variational autoencoder, decodes a
whole chunk at once. \new{It sees only the current frame.} We use the LeRobot
implementation unchanged, with all five cameras and a chunk of 100 steps
(\SI{3.3}{\second}).

\textbf{Diffusion Policy} \citep{chi2023diffusion} starts from random noise and
removes it step by step to produce a chunk, using a one-dimensional
convolutional network conditioned on the images. \new{As in the original, each
camera has its own ResNet-18 encoder; ours are trained from scratch, on images
resized to $180\times240$.} It reads the last two frames, predicts 64 steps and
executes 32, so we score the 32 it executes.

\textbf{Flow matching} is our own small policy. It keeps pi0.5's training
objective and the design of its action expert \gr{(a transformer that reads the
noisy action chunk with a time embedding, here much smaller and trained from
scratch)} but replaces the pretrained vision-language model with a ResNet-18
encoder, \new{shared by all five cameras} and trained from
scratch. Flow matching \citep{lipman2023flow} learns to move noise to an action
chunk along straight lines. \new{It sees only the current frame and plans 50
steps.} The model therefore separates pi0.5's objective from its pretraining.
\gr{Each camera frame is resized from $480\times640$ to $180\times240$ pixels,
as for Diffusion, so the encoder sees the whole image.}

\textbf{pi0.5} \citep{pi05} is a vision-language-action model: a pretrained
vision-language model reads the images and a written instruction, and a
smaller ``action expert'' produces the chunk by flow matching. \new{Its vision
encoder turns each image into tokens; it sees only the current frame.}
\rev{We finetune it with a narrow LoRA \citep{hu2022lora}, chosen to fit a
24\,GB GPU: rank 16 on the action expert's attention and state and action
layers only, about 1.3 million trained weights, with the vision-language model
frozen. Its authors finetune the whole model; Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails}
compares the published LoRA recipes.} pi0.5 has three image slots, so it reads the
overhead camera and one fingertip per arm. It is given the instruction ``fold
the short'' and plans 50 steps.

\textbf{DreamZero} \citep{dreamzero} is a world action model: it predicts
future video and future actions together, under one flow-matching objective.
\rev{The original finetunes a 14-billion-parameter pretrained video generator
(Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails}), far too large for our GPUs; ours has the
same objective and chunking but about 59 million parameters, trained from
scratch, so it tests the method, not the pretrained model.} \new{Like the original, it \emph{tiles} the cameras: all five are
squashed into the cells of a $3\times3$ grid inside one $224\times224$ frame
(Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}b), which a frozen image autoencoder compresses.} It
sees two frames, the latest and one \SI{0.8}{\second} earlier, \new{and is also
given the arm commands of the past \SI{1.6}{\second}}. It predicts frames every
\SI{0.8}{\second} up to \SI{4.8}{\second} ahead, with 24 action steps per chunk.

\textbf{FastWAM} \citep{fastwam} is also a world action model, built on a
pretrained five-billion-parameter video generator, Wan2.2 \citep{wan2025},
with a smaller action expert beside it. \new{We finetune it on our demonstrations, as its
authors do, from the public LeRobot base model.} \new{Its image input is one frame of fixed size, made by
setting cameras side by side. We give it the overhead camera beside a
two-by-two composite of the four fingertips (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}c). It sees
only the current frame.} It predicts nine frames over about a second and plans
32 action steps, of which it executes 10. \new{The authors skip the video
prediction at test time, since it is needed only in training; we run it to
score the predicted frames.}

\subsection{The demonstrations and the split}
\label{sec:data}

We recorded one task in \new{one session: all 65 demonstrations were recorded
one after another on one afternoon, in about an hour and three quarters.} A pair
of shorts, already laid flat, is folded once by the two arms. Each
demonstration lasts about 15 seconds; there are 30141 frames in all at
\SI{30}{\hertz}. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows one demonstration from
start to end.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{trajectory_strip.png}
  \caption{One held-out demonstration seen by the overhead camera, 20 frames
  evenly spaced from start to end. The arms grasp the waistband, fold the shorts
  over and release.}
  \label{fig:trajectory}
\end{figure}



We train every model on the first 58 demonstrations and test it on the
\emph{last seven}, which it never sees. \new{The last seven are also the
latest, so a slow change over the afternoon could make them harder for that
reason alone. \gr{The lighting cannot drift (the rig is in a basement lab under
constant artificial light), but the gels can wear and the operator can tire or
get faster.} To test this we retrain ACT and
Diffusion holding out a \emph{random seven} from across the session instead:
a slow drift would make those \emph{easier}, since each was recorded between
training demonstrations (Section~\ref{sec:compare}).}

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:measures}

\textbf{Action error.} We give a model one observation and compare the chunk it
plans with the commands the demonstrator actually sent next, step by step. We
take the root-mean-square error (RMSE) over all twelve joints, in degrees,
separately for each step of the chunk. \new{We do not score every frame, since
neighbouring frames are almost identical: in each demonstration we take every
25th frame as a starting point, about 18 per demonstration. That gives 1127
starting points in the 58 training demonstrations and 111 in the 7 test ones.
At each starting point the model plans one chunk, and each step of it is
compared with the recorded command at the same time.} Models plan different
distances ahead, and error grows with distance, so we compare models over the
first ten steps (\SI{0.33}{\second}), which every model plans. The \emph{gap}
is the test error divided by the training error. Models that sample have their
random seed fixed, so a score repeats exactly.

\textbf{Grad-CAM} \citep{selvaraju2017gradcam} shows where in an image a
model's output comes from. We take the magnitude of the planned chunk as the
output. We then compute its gradient with respect to the last feature map of
the ResNet encoder, average that gradient over each channel to get one weight
per channel, and sum the channels with these weights. Negative values are set
to zero, and the map is scaled so its peak is one and enlarged to the image
size. Grad-CAM needs a convolutional feature map, so it applies to ACT,
Diffusion and flow matching but not to pi0.5, whose vision encoder is a
transformer. \new{The world action models' image autoencoders are
convolutional, but they are frozen and sit before a transformer, so a Grad-CAM
map there would describe the autoencoder, not the policy.}

\new{\textbf{Patch occlusion maps} answer the same question for every model.
We divide each fingertip image into a $10\times10$ grid. For each cell in turn
we paint it with the image's own average colour, ask the model for a new plan,
and record how far the plan moved (measured as below). A map of these distances
shows which parts of the image the plan depends on. It needs only the model's
output, so it works for pi0.5 and the world action models too. For pi0.5 the
common alternative is to read the transformer's attention, but attention
weights show what the model looked at, not what changed its output.}

\textbf{Input contribution.} To ask how much a model uses one input, we replace
that input with an uninformative version and measure how far the plan moves.
An image is replaced by a flat image of its own average colour (keeping its
brightness, removing its structure), the joint positions by a constant. The
distance between two plans is the mean Euclidean distance between their
commands. Each input's distance is divided by the sum over inputs, so one
model's contributions add up to 100\,\% and rank its inputs, but cannot be
compared in size between models. Models that sample keep their seed fixed, since
sampling noise alone moves the plan more than removing a camera.

\textbf{World action models.} We score frames in two ways.
\emph{Reconstruction} passes frames the model has already seen through its own
image autoencoder and back. \emph{Prediction} compares the frames the model
generates for the future, together with its own planned actions, with what the
cameras actually recorded. \new{We use two standard image measures, and for both higher is better. The
peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) is $10\log_{10}(1/\text{MSE})$ for pixels in
$[0,1]$, in decibels (dB): every 10\,dB more means a tenfold smaller squared
pixel error, 3\,dB about half. It treats every pixel alike, so it punishes blur
and small shifts. The structural similarity index (SSIM, 0 to 1) compares local
brightness, contrast and structure, closer to what people notice. Using both
guards against a result that holds for one measure only.} We score every
sensor separately.

\new{As a baseline we \emph{repeat the last frame}: we take the last frame the
model saw and use it as the ``prediction'' for every future time. This is what
a model that has learned nothing about motion, and assumes nothing will change,
would predict. A model's \emph{margin} is its PSNR minus this baseline's. A
positive margin means the model predicts the future better than assuming
nothing moves.}

\gr{\textbf{How different the recordings look.} A held-out demonstration can be
hard for a model for two reasons: it asks for different movements, or its
images look different from those the model was trained on. This measure tests
the second reason, as each model sees the images. We pass every frame of every
demonstration through the model's own image preprocessing and vision encoder.
For each camera we keep the encoder's output for that frame as one vector of
numbers (its \emph{feature vector}): for ACT, its ResNet-18's feature map
averaged over the image; for Diffusion and flow matching, the output of that
camera's encoder; for pi0.5, its image tokens averaged; for the two world action
models, their autoencoder's latent averaged onto a coarse grid
(Appendix~\ref{sec:provenance}). Each camera's vector is scaled to length one,
so that no camera counts more than another because its encoder outputs larger
numbers, and the cameras' vectors are placed end to end to give one vector per
frame. Two frames are compared by cosine distance, one minus the cosine of the
angle between their vectors: 0 for identical directions, and the same scale
(0 to 2) for every model, so the models can be compared directly. Two
demonstrations show the same fold at different paces, so if $B$ has $M$ frames
and $A$ has $N \ge M$, we match every frame of $B$ to a different frame of $A$,
keeping the order:
\begin{equation}
  d(A,B) = \min_{i_0 < i_1 < \dots < i_{M-1}} \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}
  \bigl(1 - \cos(f^A_{i_j}, f^B_j)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a frame's feature vector; dynamic programming finds the best match
exactly. Every pair of the 65 demonstrations gives a $65\times65$ matrix. We
compute it three times per model: with all its cameras, with the overhead
camera alone, and with the fingertips alone.}

\rev{\textbf{Learning curves.} The runs above kept only their last checkpoints,
so we retrain each model with the same settings, keep a checkpoint every 5000
or \num{10000} steps, and score each as above.}


\subsection{The tactile crop}
\label{sec:cropmethod}

\new{Figure~\ref{fig:gradcamevidence} shows the Grad-CAM picture that raised the
question. On this frame, ACT puts 1.7 to 2.3 times its fair share of weight on
the outer tenth of every fingertip image, mostly along the left edge.} Such
weight near the edge could come from light leaking in at the boundary of the
gel.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{gradcam_evidence.png}
  \caption{\new{Grad-CAM of ACT on the four fingertips, frame 180 of training
  demonstration 0. Red is where the plan's gradient is largest. The dashed box
  marks the outer tenth of each image; the number is the weight inside that band
  divided by the band's share of the area (1 would be no preference).}}
  \label{fig:gradcamevidence}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rimevidence} measures what the images themselves show before
contact. On each sensor the brightest edge is 10 to 21\,\% brighter than the
centre, which fits light leaking in at the gel boundary. \new{But the edges are
not the brightest part of the image. On every sensor the brightest feature is a
vertical ridge in the gel, about 0.3 of the way across (marked in
Figure~\ref{fig:rimevidence}), up to 32\,\% brighter than the centre. So light
leakage at the edges is real but small, and the ridge is a larger bright
feature that the four-edge crop keeps.}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{rim_evidence.png}
  \caption{The four fingertip sensors before contact. Top: the first frame of
  one demonstration with the crops drawn on it: rows only (white, dashed), four
  edges (yellow) \new{and the ridge crop (blue, dash-dot); the arrow marks the
  gel's ridge.} Bottom: brightness of each row and column relative to the centre
  of the gel, averaged over the first frame of all 65 demonstrations; dotted
  lines mark the four-edge crop \new{and the blue line the ridge crop's left
  edge.}}
  \label{fig:rimevidence}
\end{figure}



We test \new{four} crops, each applied to all four fingertip images, and each
resizing the cropped image back to its original shape, which stretches it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Rows only} removes the top and bottom tenth. \new{We did not crop
  the sides at first, because on two sensors the contact marks reach the left
  and right edges of the image, so cropping columns would remove contact signal
  as well as the rim. Every sensor has spare rows.}
  \item \emph{Rows without stretching} is the same crop, but keeps the smaller
  image. Only ACT accepts cameras of different sizes, so only ACT gets it.
  \item \emph{Four edges} removes a tenth from every side.
  \item \new{\emph{Ridge crop} is the four-edge crop with its left side moved
  to the right of the ridge: it keeps rows 0.1 to 0.9 and columns 0.36 to 0.9.}
\end{itemize}
The crop is part of the model's saved input pipeline, so it is applied during
training and again at test time. We confirmed this in the saved pipeline of
every cropped model. Apart from the crop, a cropped model is identical to its
uncropped version: the same architecture, data, number of steps, batch size and
random seed.
