\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\gn{This section defines what every model is asked to do, then describes the
six models, the demonstrations they learn from and how we split them, and the
measures we use to analyse the trained models. Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
then reports the experiments.}

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\gn{\textbf{The common problem.} At time $t$ a model observes
$o_t = \big(q_t,\ I_t^{1},\dots,I_t^{C}\big)$, where $q_t\in\mathbb{R}^{12}$ is
the joint positions of both arms (five joints and a gripper per arm, which we
call \emph{proprioception}) and $I_t^{c}$ is the image from camera $c$ of the
$C$ it reads. Some models also see earlier observations (Table~\ref{tab:models},
\emph{history}). It outputs an \emph{action chunk}
$\hat A_t=(\hat a_t,\hat a_{t+1},\dots,\hat a_{t+H-1})$, a plan of the next $H$
joint commands $\hat a\in\mathbb{R}^{12}$ at \SI{30}{\hertz}, the rate at which
the demonstrations were recorded. Training teaches it to reproduce the chunk
$A_t$ the operator actually sent. Table~\ref{tab:models} lists how the models
differ, and Figure~\ref{fig:inputs} shows the images each one receives. For each
model we give its original design first and then what we changed;
Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails} gives every architecture and setting in full.}

\gn{\textbf{How the families differ.} The three behaviour-cloning policies learn
only to map observations to actions, from our demonstrations alone, with small
image encoders. pi0.5 brings a large vision-language model pretrained on web
and robot data, and is adapted to our task. The two world action models are
also trained to predict the future camera images, so that predicting how the
scene will look teaches them how it moves. All six see at most two frames, and
most see only the current one; none can recall what happened more than
\SI{1.6}{\second} earlier, a limitation we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:future}.}

\textbf{ACT} \citep{zhao2023act} \gn{reads each $480\times640$ camera image at
full resolution through one ResNet-18, shared by all five cameras and
initialised from ImageNet. The network's last feature map is a $15\times20$
grid of 512-dimensional features, and each grid cell becomes one token, with a
two-dimensional sinusoidal position embedding: 300 tokens per camera, 1500 for
all five. One more token carries the proprioception and one a latent style
variable $z$. A four-layer transformer encoder mixes all these tokens, and a
one-layer decoder turns 100 learned queries into the 100 actions of the chunk
($H=100$, \SI{3.3}{\second}), all at once. The camera and touch tokens are thus
joined simply by concatenation; every token can attend to every other. ACT is
trained as a conditional variational autoencoder: during training a second
transformer reads the proprioception and the operator's true chunk and encodes
them as a 32-dimensional Gaussian over $z$, from which $z$ is sampled, and a
Kullback--Leibler penalty (weight 10) keeps that Gaussian close to a standard
normal. The variable $z$ absorbs the variation between demonstrations, the
operator's style. At test time there is no true chunk, so $z$ is set to zero,
the mean style.} It sees only the current frame. We use the LeRobot
implementation unchanged, with all five cameras.

\textbf{Diffusion Policy} \citep{chi2023diffusion} starts from random noise and
removes it step by step to produce a chunk, using a one-dimensional
convolutional network conditioned on the images. \new{As in the original, each
camera has its own ResNet-18 encoder; ours are trained from scratch, on images
resized to $180\times240$.} It reads the last two frames, predicts 64 steps and
executes 32, so we score the 32 it executes.

\textbf{Flow matching} is our own small policy. It keeps pi0.5's training
objective and the design of its action expert \gr{(a transformer that reads the
noisy action chunk with a time embedding, here much smaller and trained from
scratch)} but replaces the pretrained vision-language model with a ResNet-18
encoder, \new{shared by all five cameras} and trained from
scratch. Flow matching \citep{lipman2023flow} learns to move noise to an action
chunk along straight lines. \new{It sees only the current frame and plans 50
steps.} The model therefore separates pi0.5's objective from its pretraining.
\gr{Each camera frame is resized from $480\times640$ to $180\times240$ pixels,
as for Diffusion, so the encoder sees the whole image.}

\textbf{pi0.5} \citep{pi05} is a vision-language-action model: a pretrained
vision-language model reads the images and a written instruction, and a
smaller ``action expert'' produces the chunk by flow matching. \new{Its vision
encoder} \gn{is a SigLIP vision transformer, which cuts each image, resized to
$224\times224$, into patches and turns each patch into a token;} \new{it sees
only the current frame.} \rev{We finetune it with a narrow LoRA
\citep{hu2022lora}, chosen to fit a 24\,GB GPU: rank 16 on the action expert's
attention and state and action layers only, about 1.3 million trained weights,
with the vision-language model frozen. Its authors finetune the whole model;}
\gn{their reference implementation needs more than 70\,GB of GPU memory for
that, an 80\,GB A100 or H100, against more than 22.5\,GB for LoRA
\citep{openpi}.} \rev{Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails} compares the published
LoRA recipes.} \gn{We also train a wide LoRA, on every attention and
feed-forward layer of both the language model and the action expert (rank 32,
53 million trained weights, batch 8), on an 80\,GB H100
(Section~\ref{sec:pi05}).} pi0.5 has three image slots, so it reads the
overhead camera and one fingertip per arm. It is given the instruction ``fold
the short'' and plans 50 steps.

\textbf{DreamZero} \citep{dreamzero} is a world action model: it predicts
future video and future actions together, under one flow-matching objective.
\rev{The original finetunes a 14-billion-parameter pretrained video generator
(Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails}), far too large for our GPUs; ours has the
same objective and chunking but about 59 million parameters, trained from
scratch, so it tests the method, not the pretrained model.} \new{Like the original, it \emph{tiles} the cameras: all five are
squashed into the cells of a $3\times3$ grid inside one $224\times224$ frame
(Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}b), which a frozen image autoencoder compresses.}
\gn{Each camera therefore gets a cell of about $75\times75$ pixels.} It
sees two frames, the latest and one \SI{0.8}{\second} earlier, \new{and is also
given the arm commands of the past \SI{1.6}{\second}}. \gn{Its actions are
planned at \SI{30}{\hertz} like every other model's; only its video is
coarser. DreamZero groups time into chunks of 48 action steps
(\SI{1.6}{\second}) and represents each chunk by two video frames, so it
predicts one frame per 24 action steps, every \SI{0.8}{\second}, up to
\SI{4.8}{\second} ahead. It executes 24 action steps (\SI{0.8}{\second}),
one video frame's worth, before planning again.}

\textbf{FastWAM} \citep{fastwam} is also a world action model, built on a
pretrained five-billion-parameter video generator, Wan2.2 \citep{wan2025},
with a smaller action expert beside it. \new{We finetune it on our demonstrations, as its
authors do, from the public LeRobot base model.} \new{Its image input is one frame of fixed size, made by
setting cameras side by side. We give it the overhead camera beside a
two-by-two composite of the four fingertips (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}c).}
\gn{The frame is $224\times448$: the overhead camera fills a $224\times224$
half, and each fingertip a $112\times112$ quarter of the other.} \new{It sees
only the current frame.} It predicts nine frames over about a second and plans
32 action steps, of which it executes 10. \new{The authors skip the video
prediction at test time, since it is needed only in training; we run it to
score the predicted frames.}

\gn{The fingertip images every model receives are therefore far coarser than
the sensors record: from $480\times640$ down to $224\times224$ for pi0.5,
$180\times240$ for Diffusion and flow matching, $112\times112$ for FastWAM and
about $75\times75$ for DreamZero; only ACT reads them at full resolution. Fine
detail of the gel's deformation, such as the texture of the cloth pressed into
it, is lost before any model sees it.}

\subsection{The demonstrations and the split}
\label{sec:data}

We recorded one task in \new{one session: all 65 demonstrations were recorded
one after another on one afternoon, in about an hour and three quarters.} A pair
of shorts, already laid flat, is folded once by the two arms. Each
demonstration lasts about 15 seconds; there are 30141 frames in all at
\SI{30}{\hertz}. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows one demonstration from
start to end.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{trajectory_strip.png}
  \caption{One held-out demonstration seen by the overhead camera, 20 frames
  evenly spaced from start to end. The arms grasp the waistband, fold the shorts
  over and release.}
  \label{fig:trajectory}
\end{figure}



We train every model on the first 58 demonstrations and test it on the
\emph{last seven}, which it never sees. \new{The last seven are also the
latest, so a slow change over the afternoon could make them harder for that
reason alone. \gr{The lighting cannot drift (the rig is in a basement lab under
constant artificial light), but the gels can wear and the operator can tire or
get faster.} To test this we retrain ACT and
Diffusion holding out a \emph{random seven} from across the session instead:
a slow drift would make those \emph{easier}, since each was recorded between
training demonstrations (Section~\ref{sec:compare}).}
