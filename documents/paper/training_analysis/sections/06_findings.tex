\section{Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

Each finding below is stated in bold, followed by the evidence already
presented and the bound on what it establishes. Nothing appears here that
cannot be read off a table or figure earlier in the report, and nothing is
stated more strongly than the sample behind it allows.

\paragraph{\textbf{On recordings it never saw, the diffusion family beats the
action-chunking family --- by far less than the obvious comparison suggests.}}
Over the horizon the two share, the margin is under a tenth; over their own
horizons it appears to be roughly a third. The direction survives the
correction and the magnitude does not. \emph{Bound:} the two families differ in
architecture and in planning horizon at once, and only the second has been
controlled for here.

\paragraph{\textbf{The two families differ more in how much they degrade than
in how well they perform.}} On familiar recordings the action-chunking family
is the more accurate of the two; on unfamiliar ones it is the less accurate. Its
error grows roughly fourfold between the two halves against under twofold for
the diffusion family. \emph{Bound:} this is the largest and most robust effect
in the report, being a ratio within each policy and immune to the horizon
problem. Section~\ref{sec:split} rules out the obvious alternative explanation:
holding back a random seven recordings instead of the last seven makes the gap
\emph{larger}, where drift across the session predicts smaller. What the
finding does not say is how much data would close it.

\paragraph{\textbf{Cropping the tactile rim buys nothing that this experiment
can detect.}} On held-out recordings both families move by about one per cent
or less, in opposite directions, on a sample too small to resolve either.
\emph{Bound:} a null result for \emph{this} crop, which removes the rim and
stretches the remainder together; it is not evidence that the rim carries no
information.

\paragraph{\textbf{The crop's effect changes sign with horizon.}} For the
action-chunking family, cropping is better over the first thirty-two steps of
the chunk and worse over the full hundred. \emph{Bound:} this is visible only
because the error is reported as a curve; both numbers come from the same pair
of runs, which is the point.

\paragraph{\textbf{Neither family over-reads the gel rim, and cropping moves
attention toward it.}} Gradient mass in the outer band sits at two thirds to
four fifths of what an indifferent model would place there, at every band width
tested, and rises for both families after cropping. This contradicts the
observation that motivated the crop. \emph{Bound:} measured on training
recordings, and the rise after cropping is partly mechanical, since removing
the outer rows moves the surviving pixels closer to the new edge.

\paragraph{\textbf{The families do not use the rig the same way, and two of
the three lean mostly on proprioception.}} The action-chunking family leans on
the overhead camera; the diffusion and fine-tuned families each take about
three quarters of their plan's movement from where the arms are. Touch ranks
last or near-last for all three. \emph{Bound:} shares are normalised within a
policy, so this is a claim about ordering and never about magnitudes; and the
fine-tuned family reads half as many tactile cameras as the others.

\paragraph{\textbf{A pooled contribution understates what touch does by about
fivefold.}} Within one held-back recording the share carried by the fingertip
cameras runs from under one per cent to over two fifths, against a pooled mean
under a tenth. The channel is very nearly ignored for seconds at a time and then
carries much of the plan. \emph{Bound:} one recording, and a contribution is a
measure of use rather than of necessity; the swing is large enough that its
direction does not depend on either.

\paragraph{\textbf{Only one of the three families reads touch in bursts.}}
The action-chunking family peaks at four to five times its own mean; the
diffusion and fine-tuned families read it steadily, peaking at two to two and
a half times theirs. \emph{Bound:} visible only framewise, on one recording,
and normalised within each policy --- so this compares each family against its
own average and never one family's magnitude against another's.

\paragraph{\textbf{A policy can be flat across its entire planning horizon on
recordings it trained on.}} The action-chunking family predicts the hundredth
step of a familiar recording about as accurately as the first. That is what
reproducing a memorised trajectory looks like, and it is not what planning
looks like. \emph{Bound:} a statement about behaviour on training data, not
about competence on the rig.

\paragraph{\textbf{The smallest generalisation gap belongs to the least-fitted
policy.}} The fine-tuned family loses only a tenth on recordings it never saw,
because its error on recordings it trained on is already above the other
families' held-out error. A small gap can mean a policy that never fitted its
training data. \emph{Bound:} one training budget of about one pass over the
data; a longer run could open the gap.

\paragraph{\textbf{Both world models plan the near term better on unseen
recordings than every policy.}} Over the ten steps all five share, their
held-out error is about a fifth below the best policy's
(Table~\ref{tab:worldactions}), and for the small one that lead is many
times its sampler's spread (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). \emph{Bound:} a third of
a second, the large model's spread not yet measured, and inputs that differ --- more past video for one, a pretrained
prior for the other --- so the objective is not isolated.

\paragraph{\textbf{The small world model predicts what the cameras will see
better than repeating the present.}} It beats holding on five or six of six
steps on every camera, most clearly on the fingertips where holding is
strongest, and its filmstrip shows it predicting a release of contact
(Table~\ref{tab:prediction}, Figures~\ref{fig:predmargin}
and~\ref{fig:filmstrip}). \emph{Bound:} one seed; its images are blurred.

\paragraph{\textbf{An image score averaged over the frame can miss a correct
prediction.}} The large world model's overhead frames move correctly and score
no better than holding, because the part that moves is small
(Figure~\ref{fig:fastwamstrip}). \emph{Bound:} one recording shown; the point
is about the metric, and it cuts in the model's favour.

One further finding follows from the control rather than from any single
measurement. \textbf{The gap is not an artefact of when the held-back
recordings were made.} A random hold-out is harder than a temporal one for both
families, and drift predicts the reverse. \emph{Bound:} the two hold-outs are
different recordings, so the two ratios are not two measurements of one
quantity; the direction is what survives.

The findings that bear most on what to do next are the generalisation gap
and the null result on cropping,
and the control above is what makes the pairing sharp. The generalisation gap
is large, robust, and now known not to be drift; the crop --- the change
actually under test --- does nothing measurable. Together they point away from
preprocessing the tactile input and towards the amount and variety of data
behind these policies.
