\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Our rig has two robot arms that fold a pair of shorts on a table. \new{We
collect data using five cameras.} One looks down on the table. The other four
are tactile sensors, one on each fingertip: each films a soft gel from behind,
so touching something shows up as a change in the gel's shading. We recorded 65
demonstrations by teleoperation. \new{In every demonstration the garment starts
in the same state and position, and the operator folds it with the same
strategy. This keeps the demonstrations consistent: in the same situation the
recorded actions differ little from one demonstration to the next (little
\emph{multi-modality}), so a model is not asked to average two different ways
of folding.}

\new{We train six state-of-the-art learning-based control policies on these
demonstrations (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item Three are \emph{behaviour-cloning} policies trained from scratch: ACT,
  Diffusion Policy and a flow-matching policy. Each learns, by imitating the
  recorded commands, to map what the robot senses to a plan of future arm
  commands.
  \item One is a \emph{vision-language-action} (VLA) model, pi0.5. It starts
  from a vision-language model pretrained on web and robot data, reads a written
  instruction, and is finetuned on our demonstrations.
  \item Two are \emph{world action models} (WAMs), DreamZero and FastWAM. They
  learn to predict future camera images together with the actions, so that
  predicting how the scene will look teaches them how it moves.
\end{itemize}}

This report answers three questions, in order of importance.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \new{\textbf{How does each policy perform on the demonstrations it was
  trained on, and on held-out demonstrations it never saw?}} This is the main
  comparison (Section~\ref{sec:compare}).
  \item \textbf{Does cropping the tactile images help?} Our earlier Grad-CAM
  pictures (defined in Section~\ref{sec:measures}) showed ACT putting weight on
  the edges of the tactile images, where light may leak in at the gel's
  boundary. We test cropping the edges away, \new{and we use Grad-CAM and patch
  occlusion maps to see whether cropping changes which parts of the tactile
  images each model relies on} (Section~\ref{sec:crop}). This question is
  secondary.
  \item \textbf{How well do the world action models predict the cameras?} We
  score how well they reproduce frames they have seen and how well they predict
  frames they have not (Section~\ref{sec:wm}).
\end{enumerate}

The main findings are these.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{On held-out demonstrations, the two world action models plan
  the next \new{ten steps (a third of a second)} most accurately.} \new{Among the
  behaviour-cloning policies, flow matching is the most accurate over those
  steps.}
  \item \textbf{Every policy is much worse on held-out demonstrations.} The gap
  is largest for ACT, which almost memorises its training demonstrations. It is
  not caused by the recording session drifting over time\rev{, nor by the
  held-out demonstrations looking unfamiliar: to every model's vision encoder
  they look as close to training demonstrations as training ones look to each
  other. The one visual change in the session, a fingertip sensor's view
  shifting at demonstration 43, falls inside the training set.}
  \item \rev{\textbf{Test error stops improving long before training ends.}
  Training error keeps falling, so the gaps are built by extra training that
  fits the training demonstrations without helping, or harming, on new ones.
  LeRobot's own held-out loss rises meanwhile and would wrongly suggest
  overfitting.}
  \item \textbf{Diffusion's plans are accurate for the first steps, but their
  error grows fastest;} ACT's grows slowly.
  \item \textbf{Cropping the tactile images changes almost nothing} on any
  model scored so far, and on average the policies do not over-weight the image
  edges.
  \item \textbf{Touch matters in short bursts.} ACT nearly ignores the tactile
  cameras most of the time and leans on them heavily around contact.
  \new{Retrained on the overhead camera alone, ACT does better on held-out
  demonstrations, so on this data the fingertips do not help it generalise.}
  \item \new{\textbf{Training pi0.5 three times longer changes almost
  nothing.} Its narrow LoRA, not too little training, is the likelier limit.}
  \item \textbf{A frame-averaged image score can miss a correct prediction} of
  a small moving part, such as a gripper.
  \item \rev{\textbf{FastWAM predicts when the gel will be touched from the
  image alone.} It foresees the contact of a grasp at the right moment, and
  telling it the gripper is already closed changes nothing. Retrained so that
  its predicted video reads the commands, it predicts the touch more accurately,
  and predicts none when the grasp is not commanded.}
  \item \rev{\textbf{DreamZero hardly reads its cameras to plan:} all five
  together move its plan by about 2\,\%; proprioception and its own past
  commands carry the rest.}
\end{itemize}

them. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what we learned and what to do next.
