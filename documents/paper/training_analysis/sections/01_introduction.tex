\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Our rig has two robot arms that fold a pair of shorts on a table. \new{We
collect data using five cameras.} One looks down on the table. The other four
are tactile sensors, one on each fingertip: each films a soft gel from behind,
so touching something shows up as a change in the gel's shading. We recorded 65
demonstrations by teleoperation. \new{In every demonstration the garment starts
in the same state and position, and the operator folds it with the same
strategy. This keeps the demonstrations consistent: in the same situation the
recorded actions differ little from one demonstration to the next (little
\emph{multi-modality}), so a model is not asked to average two different ways
of folding.}

\new{We train six state-of-the-art learning-based control policies on these
demonstrations (Section~\ref{sec:models}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item Three are \emph{behaviour-cloning} policies trained from scratch: ACT,
  Diffusion Policy and a flow-matching policy. Each learns, by imitating the
  recorded commands, to map what the robot senses to a plan of future arm
  commands.
  \item One is a \emph{vision-language-action} (VLA) model, pi0.5. It starts
  from a vision-language model pretrained on web and robot data, reads a written
  instruction, and is finetuned on our demonstrations.
  \item Two are \emph{world action models} (WAMs), DreamZero and FastWAM. They
  learn to predict future camera images together with the actions, so that
  predicting how the scene will look teaches them how it moves.
\end{itemize}}

This report answers three questions, in order of importance.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \new{\textbf{How does each policy perform on the demonstrations it was
  trained on, and on held-out demonstrations it never saw?}} This is the main
  comparison (Section~\ref{sec:compare}).
  \item \gn{\textbf{Does naive integration of tactile images help to improve the
  policy performance?} By \emph{naive} we mean that the fingertip images are
  given to each model as further camera images, with no tactile-specific
  encoder or fusion. We retrain every model without them, measure how much each
  model's plan depends on them, and test whether the bright edges of the gel
  images mislead the models (Section~\ref{sec:sensors}).}
  \item \textbf{How well do the world action models predict \gn{the future
  observations}?} We score how well they reproduce frames they have seen and how
  well they predict frames they have not (Section~\ref{sec:wm}).
\end{enumerate}

\gn{Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews how touch has been brought into robot
learning. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the models, the data and the
measures we use, Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what we learned, and
Section~\ref{sec:future} proposes what to do next. The appendices give each
model's full architecture and settings and explain how every figure and table
was made.}

\gn{The main findings are these, grouped by question.}

\gn{\textbf{Question 1: performance on seen and unseen demonstrations.}}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \gr{\textbf{The two world action models plan best on demonstrations
  they never saw.} We measure how far each model's planned arm commands are
  from the commands the operator actually sent, over the next ten steps (a
  third of a second). On the held-out demonstrations FastWAM and DreamZero have
  the smallest error of all six models (Table~\ref{tab:actions}); flow matching
  has the smallest of the three behaviour-cloning policies.}
  \item \gn{\textbf{In general, every model is much worse on held-out
  demonstrations than on the ones it trained on.}} The gap is largest for ACT,
  which almost memorises its training demonstrations.
  \item \rev{\textbf{Test error stops improving long before training ends}
  while training error keeps falling, for every model but FastWAM
  \gr{(Figure~\ref{fig:curves})}; the extra training neither helps nor harms
  on new demonstrations.}
  \item \gn{\textbf{pi0.5 was held back by its narrow LoRA, not by too little
  training.} Training it three times longer changes almost nothing, but
  adapting forty times more of its weights, at a batch of eight, cuts its test
  error over the first ten steps from 9.0 to 4.6 degrees, close to the best
  models (Section~\ref{sec:pi05}).}
\end{itemize}

\gn{\textbf{Question 2: does naive touch help?}}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \gn{\textbf{Touch matters only around contact.} ACT nearly ignores the
  tactile cameras most of the time and leans on them heavily when the gripper
  closes on the cloth.}
  \item \new{\textbf{Retrained on the overhead camera alone, ACT does better on
  held-out demonstrations}, so on this data the fingertips do not help it
  generalise.} \gn{\pending{overhead-only result for the other five models.}}
  \item \rev{\textbf{DreamZero hardly reads its cameras to plan:} all five
  together move its plan by about \gr{7}\,\%; proprioception and its own past
  commands carry the rest.}
  \item \textbf{Cropping the tactile images changes almost nothing} on any
  model, and no model over-weights the image edges.
\end{itemize}

\gn{\textbf{Question 3: predicting the future observations.}}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \gn{\textbf{An image score averaged over the whole frame can miss a
  correct prediction of a small moving part.} The grippers cover only a few per
  cent of the overhead image, so FastWAM, which predicts their motion
  correctly, scores no better than simply repeating the last frame, which gets
  the static background right and the grippers wrong (Section~\ref{sec:wm}).}
\end{itemize}
