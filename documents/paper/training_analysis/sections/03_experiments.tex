\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We report four experiments, in the order of the questions in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Section~\ref{sec:compare} compares all the models,
Section~\ref{sec:crop} tests the tactile crop, Section~\ref{sec:sensors} asks
which sensors ACT needs, and Section~\ref{sec:wm} scores the world models'
predictions of the cameras.

\textbf{Both world models plan the next third of a second better than every
policy on unseen demonstrations.} Over the first ten steps their test errors
are 3.9 (FastWAM) and 4.2 (DreamZero), against 5.0 for the best policy,
Diffusion. The two world models are too close to rank. Their lead is several
times larger than the change we see when we rerun them with different random
seeds (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:floor}). It does not prove that predicting the
future is what helps: DreamZero also sees more past frames than any policy, and
FastWAM starts from a pretrained video model.

\begin{newtext}
\textbf{Diffusion is the most accurate policy for the first steps of a plan,
but its error grows fastest.} On the test demonstrations its error starts at
3.9 and reaches 13.9 by step 32, a factor of 3.6. ACT starts higher, at 7.3,
but grows by only a factor of 1.8 over the same steps, so the two lines cross
at about one second (Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}, left). On the training
demonstrations the difference is starker. ACT's error is almost flat across all
100 steps (2.4 to 3.3), which means it reproduces the rest of a familiar
demonstration almost exactly. Diffusion's error grows sixfold over its 32
steps.
\end{newtext}

\textbf{Every model is much worse on unseen demonstrations.} ACT's error is
about five times higher on the test demonstrations than on the training ones,
and Diffusion's about twice as high. A natural worry is that the last seven
demonstrations are harder because the session drifted, for example in lighting
or gel wear. Figure~\ref{fig:split} rules this out. \new{When we hold out seven
demonstrations picked at random from across the session, the gap becomes
larger, not smaller. A random test set lies between training demonstrations in
time, so drift should make it easier; it is harder.} The two test sets are
different demonstrations, so only the direction of this change is certain, not
its size (Appendix~\ref{sec:threats}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{split_comparison.png}
  \caption{The gap (test error divided by training error) when testing on the
  last seven demonstrations and on a random seven. The dotted line is a model
  equally good on both.}
  \label{fig:split}
\end{figure}

\textbf{pi0.5's small gap does not mean it generalises well.} Its test error is
only a tenth above its training error, but its error on the demonstrations it
trained on is already higher than Diffusion's error on demonstrations it never
saw. It trained for about one pass over the data, adapting only the small LoRA
addition, so it never fitted the training set.

\subsection{Cropping the tactile images}
\label{sec:crop}

Table~\ref{tab:crop} gives the test error of every model with and without each
crop.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\new{Test action error (RMSE over each model's own plan) with each
  tactile crop. \emph{Rows} removes the top and bottom tenth and stretches the
  rest back; \emph{rows, unstretched} keeps the cropped size (ACT only);
  \emph{four edges} removes a tenth from every side. Each entry is one training
  run.}}
  \label{tab:crop}
  \small
  \input{tables/crop_table}
\end{table}

\textbf{Cropping the tactile images changes almost nothing.} With the rows-only
crop, ACT, Diffusion and pi0.5 move by a few per cent at most, in both
directions. That is within what repeating a run with another random seed moves
Diffusion and pi0.5 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:floor}). \pending{four-edge crop
on all five models.} For ACT, the one place the rows-only crop looked harmful,
over the far end of its plan, disappears when the image is not stretched. So
the stretching, not the loss of the rim, caused that small penalty.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{rim_attention.png}
  \caption{How much Grad-CAM weight falls in a band around the edge of the
  tactile images, relative to a completely flat map (dashed line at 1), over
  60 frames of test demonstration 58 and all four fingertips. Below
  the line, a model looks at the edge \emph{less} than a model that ignored
  position would. Three band widths are shown.}
  \label{fig:rim}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The policies do not over-weight the edges of the tactile images.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rim} measures this directly. \new{A value of 1 means the edge
band gets exactly its share of the weight by area; above 1, the model favours
the edge.} \new{On a test demonstration,} every uncropped model sits between two thirds
and nine tenths of its share, at every band width. So the edge attention we saw in single Grad-CAM pictures
does not hold on average. Cropping moves the weight slightly towards the new
edge, partly for a mechanical reason: after cropping and stretching, the pixels
nearest the edge used to be further in. \pending{rim measure for the
four-edge crop.}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{framewise_shares.png}
  \caption{The fingertips' share of each policy's plan through one test
  demonstration. Dashed lines are the cropped versions.}
  \label{fig:framewise}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Touch matters in bursts.} Figure~\ref{fig:framewise} tracks the
fingertips' share through one test demonstration. For ACT it runs from under
1\,\% to 44\,\% within ten seconds, against an average of 10\,\%. It is nearly
ignored for the first three seconds and carries two fifths of the plan around
contact. An average therefore describes no moment that actually happens.
Diffusion and pi0.5 read touch more steadily, peaking at two to two and a half
times their own average where ACT peaks at four to five times.

\subsection{How well the world models predict the cameras}
\label{sec:wm}

\begin{newtext}
Table~\ref{tab:wm} scores both world models on every sensor. The first column
is reconstruction, the frames the model saw passed through its own
autoencoder, which is the sharpest any of its predictions can be. The next
columns compare its predicted future frames with the real ones and with
repeating the last frame it saw.
\end{newtext}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\new{World models on the seven test demonstrations, per sensor, in
  PSNR (dB). \emph{Reconstruction}: seen frames through the model's own
  autoencoder. \emph{Prediction} and \emph{repeat last}: future frames,
  averaged over the prediction horizon. \emph{Margin}: prediction minus repeat
  last, at \SI{0.8}{\second} ahead and at the end of each model's horizon
  (\SI{4.8}{\second} for DreamZero, \SI{1.1}{\second} for FastWAM). Each model
  works at its own resolution, so compare margins, not PSNR, between models.}}
  \label{tab:wm}
  \small
  \input{tables/wm_table}
\end{table}

\begin{newtext}
\textbf{Most of the prediction error comes from predicting, not from
compressing the images.} DreamZero's small image autoencoder is the exception
on the overhead camera: it reproduces a seen frame at only 17.3\,dB, and the
model's predictions reach 15.5\,dB, close to that ceiling. On the fingertips
its autoencoder reaches 34 to 37\,dB, but its predictions only 25 to 30\,dB.
FastWAM's pretrained autoencoder is much sharper, 34\,dB on the overhead camera
and about 46\,dB on the fingertips, and its predictions sit 13 to 17\,dB below
that. So a better autoencoder would remove little of either model's error,
except DreamZero's on the overhead camera. The two models work at different
image resolutions, so their PSNR values should not be compared directly; their
margins over repeating the last frame can be.
\end{newtext}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{prediction_margin.png}
  \caption{Each world model's PSNR minus that of repeating the last frame,
  against how far ahead the frame is. Above zero, the model predicts better than
  assuming nothing moves.}
  \label{fig:predmargin}
\end{figure}

\textbf{DreamZero predicts the cameras better than repeating the last frame,
on every sensor, from \SI{1.6}{\second} ahead onwards.} Its margin is roughly 3
to 8\,dB, and largest on the fingertips, where repeating the last frame is
already strong because the gels change only at contact. At \SI{0.8}{\second} it
only ties, because the future still looks almost exactly like the present.
Figure~\ref{fig:filmstrip} shows one example: the model predicts the arms
lifting and the contact marks on a gel fading, as really happened, though its
images are blurred.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{dreamzero_filmstrip.png}
  \caption{One test moment as DreamZero predicted it. For each camera the rows
  are what happened, what the model predicted, and the last frame it saw;
  columns are \SI{0.8}{\second} apart.}
  \label{fig:filmstrip}
\end{figure}

\textbf{FastWAM predicts sharp, correctly moving frames, but a frame-averaged
score barely rewards them.} It predicts only about a second ahead. On the
overhead camera it ties with repeating the last frame, although its frames show
the grippers moving at the right pace (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:fastwamstrip}).
The garment fills most of the frame and hardly moves within a second, so
repeating the last frame is already right about most pixels. \textbf{A score
averaged over the whole frame can rate a correct prediction of a small moving
part as no better than doing nothing.} On the fingertips, where contact changes
many pixels, FastWAM starts below repeating the last frame and ends 3 to 6\,dB
above it on every sensor. At the
one moment both models predict, \SI{0.8}{\second} ahead, FastWAM is ahead on
both panels of Figure~\ref{fig:predmargin}.
