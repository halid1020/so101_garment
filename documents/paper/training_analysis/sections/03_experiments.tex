\subsection{\bl{\gn{Evaluation measures}}}
\label{sec:measures}

\textbf{Action error.} We give a model one observation and compare the chunk it
plans with the commands the demonstrator actually sent next, step by step. We
take the root-mean-square error (RMSE) over all twelve joints, in degrees,
separately for each step of the chunk. \new{We do not score every frame, since
neighbouring frames are almost identical: in each demonstration we take every
25th frame as a starting point, about 18 per demonstration. That gives 1127
starting points in the 58 training demonstrations and 111 in the 7 test ones.
At each starting point the model plans one chunk, and each step of it is
compared with the recorded command at the same time.} Models plan different
distances ahead, and error grows with distance, so we compare models over the
first ten steps (\SI{0.33}{\second}), which every model plans. The \emph{gap}
is the test error divided by the training error. Models that sample have their
random seed fixed, so a score repeats exactly. \gn{\bl{Action error is an
approximate, offline measure of how well a policy performs.} \bl{It asks how closely
the policy reproduces what the operator did, not whether the garment ends up
folded: a lower action error does not necessarily mean better performance on
the robot, nor a higher one worse, since a plan can differ from the
demonstration and still fold the garment, or match it closely and fail at the
one moment that matters.}}

\textbf{Grad-CAM} \citep{selvaraju2017gradcam} shows where in an image a
model's output comes from. We take the magnitude of the planned chunk as the
output. We then compute its gradient with respect to the last feature map of
the ResNet encoder, average that gradient over each channel to get one weight
per channel, and sum the channels with these weights. Negative values are set
to zero, and the map is scaled so its peak is one and enlarged to the image
size. \bl{Grad-CAM needs a convolutional feature map, so it applies to ACT,
Diffusion and flow matching but not to pi0.5, whose vision encoder is a
\gn{SigLIP vision transformer: it represents an image as a sequence of patch
tokens, with no convolutional feature map to weight.}} \gn{\bl{The world action
models first compress each camera frame with an image autoencoder, a
convolutional network that was pretrained elsewhere and is kept frozen while
the rest of the model trains.} \bl{Frozen means its weights do not change; it does
not stop a gradient from passing through it.} \bl{So we can still ask how the
planned chunk would change if the autoencoder's internal picture of the frame
changed: we run the model forward, from the frame through the autoencoder, the
transformer and the denoising steps that produce the plan, and then follow the
gradient of the plan all the way back to the autoencoder's last feature map.}
\bl{That map covers the whole tiled frame, so we cut it into the cameras' regions
and treat each region as one camera's Grad-CAM.} \bl{The map is coarse: for FastWAM
it is $14\times28$ cells, so each fingertip gets only $7\times7$.}
\bl{Figure~\ref{fig:rim} reports where these maps put their weight.}}

\new{\textbf{Patch occlusion maps} answer the same question for every model.
We divide each fingertip image into a $10\times10$ grid. For each cell in turn
we paint it with the image's own average colour, ask the model for a new plan,
and record how far the plan moved (measured as below). A map of these distances
shows which parts of the image the plan depends on. It needs only the model's
output, so it works for pi0.5 and the world action models too. For pi0.5 the
common alternative is to read the transformer's attention, but attention
weights show what the model looked at, not what changed its output.}
\gr{\bl{We paint a cell with the image's average colour rather than with black
because a black square is something the model never saw in training: a gel
image is never black, so the plan would move partly because the input is
strange, not because the information under the cell is gone.} \bl{The average colour
keeps the cell's brightness and removes its detail.}}

\textbf{Input contribution.} To ask how much a model uses one input, we replace
that input with an uninformative version and measure how far the plan moves.
\bl{An image is replaced by a flat image of its own average colour (keeping its
brightness, removing its structure).} \gr{\bl{The joint positions are replaced by
their average over the training demonstrations, joint by joint: a typical
posture, which removes what the current posture says without handing the model a
pose it never saw.} \bl{Replacing everything with zeros instead (a black image, and
every joint at zero degrees, a folded posture the arms never took) asks how the
model reacts to impossible inputs, so we use it only as a check
(Appendix~\ref{sec:extra}).}} \gn{\bl{Formally, let $\hat A_t$ be the plan from the real observation and
$\hat A_t^{(k)}$ the plan with input $k$ replaced.} \bl{The distance between the two
plans is the mean Euclidean distance between their commands,}
\begin{equation}
  d_k(t) = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\bigl\|\hat a_{t+h}-\hat a^{(k)}_{t+h}\bigr\|_2 ,
\end{equation}
\bl{and input $k$'s contribution at that observation is its share of the total,
$s_k(t)=d_k(t)/\sum_{j} d_j(t)$, averaged over the scored observations $t$ and
given in per cent.}} \bl{So one model's contributions add up to 100\,\% and rank its
inputs, but cannot be compared in size between models.} \bl{Models that sample keep their seed fixed, since
sampling noise alone moves the plan more than removing a camera.}

\textbf{World action models.} We score frames in two ways.
\emph{Reconstruction} passes frames the model has already seen through its own
image autoencoder and back. \emph{Prediction} compares the frames the model
generates for the future, together with its own planned actions, with what the
cameras actually recorded. \new{We use two standard image measures, and for both higher is better. The
peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) is $10\log_{10}(1/\text{MSE})$ for pixels in
$[0,1]$, in decibels (dB): every 10\,dB more means a tenfold smaller squared
pixel error, 3\,dB about half. It treats every pixel alike, so it punishes blur
and small shifts. The structural similarity index (SSIM, 0 to 1) compares local
brightness, contrast and structure, closer to what people notice. Using both
guards against a result that holds for one measure only.} We score every
sensor separately.

\new{As a baseline we \emph{repeat the last frame}: we take the last frame the
model saw and use it as the ``prediction'' for every future time. This is what
a model that has learned nothing about motion, and assumes nothing will change,
would predict. A model's \emph{margin} is its PSNR minus this baseline's. A
positive margin means the model predicts the future better than assuming
nothing moves.}

\gr{\bl{\textbf{How different the recordings look.}} \bl{A held-out demonstration can be
hard for a model for two reasons: it asks for different movements, or its
images look different from those the model was trained on.} \bl{This measure tests
the second reason, as each model sees the images.} \bl{We pass every frame of every
demonstration through the model's own image preprocessing and vision encoder.}
\gn{\bl{For each camera $c$ we summarise the encoder's output for that frame as one
vector of numbers, its \emph{feature vector} $f^{c}$, taken at the last point
where the model still holds one summary per image:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \bl{ACT: its ResNet-18's $15\times20\times512$ feature map averaged over
  the 300 positions, a 512-number vector;}
  \item \bl{Diffusion and flow matching: the output of that camera's encoder (the
  image's keypoints after a linear layer), which is what conditions the action
  network;}
  \item \bl{pi0.5: its 256 image tokens after the projection into the language
  model, averaged into one token;}
  \item \bl{the two world action models: their autoencoder's latent image averaged
  onto a coarse grid ($6\times6$ for DreamZero, two cells per camera tile;
  $4\times8$ for FastWAM), and split by the grid cells that fall in each
  camera's tile.}
\end{itemize}
\bl{Each camera's vector is scaled to length one, $\tilde f^{c}=f^{c}/\|f^{c}\|$,
so that no camera counts more than another because its encoder outputs larger
numbers, and the cameras' vectors are joined end to end into one vector per
frame, $f=(\tilde f^{1},\dots,\tilde f^{C})$.}} \bl{Two frames are compared by cosine distance, one minus the cosine of the
angle between their vectors: 0 for identical directions.} \gr{\bl{Raw cosine
distances are not comparable between models, because an encoder whose features
all point in nearly the same direction (pi0.5's averaged image tokens) puts every
pair close to zero.} \bl{So each model's distances are expressed in units of its own
typical training gap, the average distance from a training demonstration to its
nearest training neighbour: 1 then means ``as different as two training
demonstrations usually are'' for every model.}} \gn{\bl{With $\mathcal{T}$ the
training demonstrations and $d$ the distance of Equation~\eqref{eq:dp} below,}
\begin{equation}
  \bar g = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{T}}\ \min_{B\in\mathcal{T},\,B\neq A} d(A,B),
  \qquad
  \tilde d(A,B) = \frac{d(A,B)}{\bar g}.
\end{equation}
\bl{For a test demonstration the question is then whether its nearest training
demonstration is further away than that, $\min_{B\in\mathcal{T}}\tilde d(A,B)>1$.}} \bl{Two
demonstrations show the same fold at different paces, so if $B$ has $M$ frames
and $A$ has $N \ge M$, we match every frame of $B$ to a different frame of $A$,
keeping the order:}
\begin{equation}
  d(A,B) = \min_{i_0 < i_1 < \dots < i_{M-1}} \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}
  \bigl(1 - \cos(f^A_{i_j}, f^B_j)\bigr),
  \label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
\bl{where $f$ is a frame's feature vector; dynamic programming finds the best match
exactly.} \bl{Every pair of the 65 demonstrations gives a $65\times65$ matrix.} \bl{We
compute it three times per model: with all its cameras, with the overhead
camera alone, and with the fingertips alone.}}

\rev{\bl{\textbf{Learning curves.}} \bl{The runs above kept only their last checkpoints,
so we retrain each model with the same settings, keep a checkpoint every 5000
or \num{10000} steps, and score each as above.}}

\rev{\bl{\textbf{Flow matching is the most accurate behaviour-cloning policy}, over
the first ten steps and further ahead: 4.3 against Diffusion's 5.0 over ten
steps, 7.3 against 7.6 over 24 and 9.0 against 9.2 over 32.} \bl{Flow matching and
pi0.5 share their training objective, and flow matching's error over the first
ten steps is half pi0.5's (4.3 against 9.0): on our data, pi0.5's pretraining
with our narrow LoRA does worse than a small model trained from scratch}}
\gn{\bl{; with a wide LoRA it comes close (4.6, Section~\ref{sec:pi05}).}
\bl{Unlike ACT, flow matching trains its ResNet-18 from scratch.} \bl{Retrained from
ACT's ImageNet weights, with the encoder's batch normalisation kept as
pretrained weights require, it is worse, not better: its training error doubles
(9.9 against 4.8 over its plan) and its test error rises from 11.5 to 13.1 (4.5
against 4.3 over the first ten steps).} \bl{With the encoder's learning rate kept at
a tenth of the rest, as in the from-scratch run, the pretrained features seem
too slow to adapt to gel images and an overhead view of cloth; one run cannot
say whether another learning rate would reverse this.} \bl{Its
sampler takes 10 Euler steps from noise to a plan.} \bl{Scored with only 4, without
retraining, it is no worse: 11.5 over its plan, as with 10, and 4.1 against 4.3
over the first ten steps.} \bl{Flow matching is trained to follow straight
paths from noise to action, which plausibly need few integration steps; the
action expert could then be run 4 times per plan instead of 10.}}

\textbf{Diffusion's error starts low but grows fastest.} On the test
demonstrations it rises from 3.9 at the first step to 13.9 by step 32, a factor
of 3.6; ACT starts at 7.3 but grows by only 1.8, so the two cross at about one
second (Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}). On the training demonstrations ACT's error is
almost flat over all 100 steps (2.4 to 3.3): it replays a familiar
demonstration almost exactly.

\textbf{Every model is much worse on unseen demonstrations.} ACT's error is
about five times higher on the test demonstrations than on the training ones,
and Diffusion's about twice as high. \new{Because the last seven demonstrations
are also the latest, a slow change over the afternoon could make them harder.
When the seven are instead picked at random, each sits between training
demonstrations in time, so drift should make them easier; the gap grows instead
(8.9 against 5.3 for ACT, 2.6 against 1.9 for Diffusion; Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:split}). Drift does not explain the gap.}

\textbf{pi0.5's small gap does not mean it generalises well.} Its error on
the demonstrations it trained on is already higher than Diffusion's on
demonstrations it never saw: after about one pass over the data, adapting only
the small LoRA addition, it never fitted the training set. \new{\bl{Trained for
about three passes (\num{70000} steps), it barely moved: training error fell
from 17.1 to 16.4 and test error from 18.4 to 18.0, less than rescoring with
another seed moves it (Table~\ref{tab:floor}).} The narrow LoRA, not
too little training, is the likelier limit.}

\bl{\textbf{The test error stops improving long before training ends; the training
error does not.}} \bl{Diffusion's test error levels off at about 5.2 by
\num{40000} of its \num{100000} steps, while its training error keeps falling,
from 3.5 to 1.8.} \bl{Flow matching's levels off by \num{20000} of \num{60000}.}
\bl{ACT's training error falls from 8.4 to 2.5 over the whole run, while its test
error moves only from 10.5 to about 8, and over its whole plan not at all (14.7
at \num{5000} steps, 14.3 at the end).} \bl{The large gaps of
Table~\ref{tab:actions} are therefore built late in training: the extra steps
fit the training demonstrations more closely without helping on new ones.}
\bl{They do not make the test error worse either, so stopping earlier would save
time, not improve the models.}

\bl{\textbf{pi0.5 never pulls apart.}} \bl{Its training and test errors fall together
and level off at about \num{20000} steps, with a gap of 1.1 throughout: it
stops learning rather than overfitting, as Section~\ref{sec:compare} found.}

\bl{\textbf{The trainer's own held-out loss would have misled us.}} \bl{LeRobot also
reports each model's training loss on the test demonstrations.} \bl{For Diffusion
it rises fourfold from \num{10000} steps (0.029 to 0.119), and for flow
matching it rises too, which reads as severe overfitting, yet neither model's
test action error gets worse.} \bl{These losses score how well a model removes
noise from the recorded actions, not how close its plan is to them.}

\bl{\textbf{The world action models differ.}} \bl{DreamZero reaches its best test error
by \num{20000} of its \num{80000} steps (3.9) and then drifts up slightly, to
4.1, while its training error falls from 1.7 to 0.8; its held-out loss rises
2.5-fold.} \bl{FastWAM is the only model still improving at the end: its test error
falls from 4.6 to 4.1 to 3.9 over its \num{30000} steps, so it would probably
gain from longer training, although its held-out loss rises here too.}}
\gn{\bl{We therefore trained FastWAM for \num{80000} steps, about 23 passes over the
training frames as for ACT and DreamZero.} \bl{Its test error is 3.6 against 3.9, and its training error stays at 1.5.}
\bl{But scoring the \num{30000}-step model with three random seeds already spans
3.56 to 3.74 (Table~\ref{tab:floor}), and 3.6 falls inside that range, so the
longer training does not measurably help.}}

\gr{\subsubsection{\bl{Why pi0.5 does poorly with our LoRA}}
\label{sec:pi05}

\bl{pi0.5 is the one model that never fits its own training demonstrations: its
training error (17.1 over its plan; 8.3 over the first ten steps in its
learning-curve run) is higher than
every other model's \emph{test} error over those ten steps, and its learning
curve levels off by \num{20000} steps with the training and test errors moving
together (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}).} \bl{That is under-fitting, not over-fitting, and
\gn{three} things in how we trained it point to it.}

\bl{\textbf{It saw each training frame about once.}} \bl{We trained with a batch of one
(to fit a 24\,GB GPU), so \num{30000} steps show the model each of the
\num{27465} training frames 1.1 times on average.} \bl{The other models see each
frame 9 (FastWAM) to 35 (flow matching) times.} \bl{Training \num{70000} steps
(2.5 passes) barely moved it (Section~\ref{sec:compare}), but that is still
far fewer passes, each with a batch of one.}

\bl{\textbf{It could change very little.}} \bl{Our LoRA trains rank-16 corrections to
the action expert's query and value projections and to its state and action
layers: about 1.3 million weights, 0.03\,\% of the model.} \bl{The vision-language
model that turns the images and the instruction into the tokens the action
expert reads stays exactly as pretrained.} \bl{Published recipes adapt far more
(Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails}).}

\bl{\textbf{Its inputs are new to it.}} \bl{Two of its three image slots were pretrained
on wrist cameras; we feed them fingertip images of a gel.} \bl{With the
vision-language model frozen, these reach the action expert only through
features never trained on anything like them.}

\bl{\textbf{What should help}, in order of cost: a larger batch and more passes;
LoRA on the attention and feed-forward layers of both the language model and
the action expert, as in the openpi recipe; and full finetuning, which needs far
more than 24\,GB of GPU memory or sharding over several GPUs.} \bl{We tested the first
two together: a rank-32 LoRA on both parts, trained with a batch of 8 for the
same \num{30000} steps (8.7 passes).}}

\gn{\bl{\textbf{The wide LoRA removes most of the deficit.}} \bl{With 53 million trained
weights instead of 1.3 million, and eight times the batch, pi0.5 now fits its
training demonstrations: its training error over its plan falls from 17.1 to
4.9, and its test error from 18.4 to 12.8 (Table~\ref{tab:actions}).} \bl{Over the
first ten steps its test error falls from 9.0 to 4.6, behind only FastWAM (3.9),
DreamZero (4.2) and flow matching (4.3), and ahead of Diffusion (5.0) and ACT
(7.9).} \bl{Like every model that fits its training set, it now shows a gap between
training and test (2.6 over its plan, against 1.1 before).} \bl{So the narrow
adapter, not the pretrained model, was what held pi0.5 back.} \bl{With only one
setting tried, we cannot say how much of the gain comes from the wider adapter
and how much from the larger batch; full finetuning, which would need sharding
over several GPUs or an 80\,GB card at a small batch, remains untested.}}

\subsection{Which sensors the models use}
\label{sec:sensors}

\gn{\bl{Every model here receives the fingertip images naively: as further camera
images, through the same encoder as the overhead camera or a copy of it, with
no tactile-specific processing.} \bl{We ask whether that helps in three ways.} \bl{The
direct test is to retrain each model without the fingertips and compare its
action error.} \bl{The second measures how much each trained model's plan depends
on each input.} \bl{The third asks whether the bright edges of the gel images
mislead the models (Section~\ref{sec:crop}).}

\bl{\textbf{Retraining without touch.}}} We retrained ACT with three sets of inputs,
all with proprioception: the overhead camera alone; the overhead camera and one
fingertip per arm (the pair pi0.5 reads); and all five cameras.
\bl{\gn{We retrained each of the other five models on the overhead camera alone,
with the same settings as its main run.}} Table~\ref{tab:sensors} gives the
result. \bl{\new{The overhead-only model tests the reading of Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:framewise}: if \gn{a model} uses touch only briefly, removing
touch altogether should cost little.}}

  \centering

\gn{\subsubsection{\bl{Do the bright edges of the gel images mislead the models?}}
\label{sec:crop}
\label{sec:cropmethod}

\bl{\textbf{Why we tested cropping.}} \bl{An early Grad-CAM picture of ACT
(Figure~\ref{fig:gradcamevidence}) put 1.7 to 2.3 times its fair share of weight
on the outer tenth of every fingertip image.} \bl{If light leaks in at the boundary of
the gel, the edges of a tactile image change with lighting and with the gel's
seating rather than with contact, and a model that keys on them would learn the
wrong signal.} \bl{Optical tactile sensors are known to be lit unevenly at the
boundary, and calibration pipelines crop it away (Section~\ref{sec:rw-hard}).}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{gradcam_evidence.png}
  \caption{\new{Grad-CAM of ACT on the four fingertips, frame 180 of training
  demonstration 0. Red is where the plan's gradient is largest. The dashed box
  marks the outer tenth of each image; the number is the weight inside that band
  divided by the band's share of the area (1 would be no preference).}}
  \label{fig:gradcamevidence}
\end{figure}

\bl{\textbf{What the images show.}} \bl{Before contact, each sensor's brightest edge is
10 to 21\,\% brighter than the centre of the gel (Figure~\ref{fig:rimevidence}),
consistent with a small leak.} \bl{But the brightest feature on every sensor is not an
edge: it is a vertical ridge in the gel about 0.3 of the way across, up to
32\,\% brighter than the centre.}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{rim_evidence.png}
  \caption{The four fingertip sensors before contact. Top: the first frame of
  one demonstration with the crops drawn on it: rows only (white, dashed), four
  edges (yellow) \new{and the ridge crop (blue, dash-dot); the arrow marks the
  gel's ridge.} Bottom: brightness of each row and column relative to the centre
  of the gel, averaged over the first frame of all 65 demonstrations; dotted
  lines mark the four-edge crop \new{and the blue line the ridge crop's left
  edge.}}
  \label{fig:rimevidence}
\end{figure}

\bl{\textbf{The four crops.}} \bl{Each crop is applied to all four fingertip images, in
training and again at test time, as part of the model's saved input pipeline;
everything else about the model (architecture, data, steps, batch, seed) is
unchanged.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Rows only} removes the top and bottom tenth. \bl{It leaves the sides,
  because on two sensors the contact marks reach the left and right edges.}
  \item \bl{\emph{Rows, unstretched} is the same crop without resizing the image
  back to its original shape.} Only ACT accepts cameras of different sizes, so
  only ACT gets it. \bl{It separates removing the rim from stretching the image.}
  \item \emph{Four edges} removes a tenth from every side.
  \item \bl{\emph{Ridge crop} also removes the ridge: it keeps rows 0.1 to 0.9 and
  columns 0.36 to 0.9.}
\end{itemize}

\bl{\textbf{Result 1: cropping does not change accuracy.}} \bl{No crop moves any model's

(Table~\ref{tab:crop}), within what rescoring with another random seed moves it

\subsection{\bl{How well the world action models predict \gn{the future observations}}}
\label{sec:wm}

\gn{\bl{A world action model is trained to predict what its cameras will see, as
well as what the arms should do.} \bl{This section asks how good those predictions
are.} \bl{Table~\ref{tab:wm} scores both models on every sensor of the test
demonstrations, with three numbers per sensor:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \bl{\emph{Reconstruction}: a frame the model has seen, compressed by its own
  image autoencoder and decompressed again.} \bl{No prediction can be sharper than
  this, so it is the model's ceiling.} \bl{For DreamZero the autoencoder is a frozen
  image autoencoder applied to each frame; for FastWAM it is Wan2.2's video
  autoencoder, which compresses groups of four frames together.}
  \item \bl{\emph{Prediction}: the future frames the model generates, compared with
  what the cameras actually recorded.}
  \item \bl{\emph{Repeat last}: the score of simply copying the last seen frame
  into every future time.} \bl{This is the floor: a model that has learned nothing
  about motion would score this, so only a prediction above it shows that the
  model predicts change.} \bl{The difference is the model's \emph{margin}.}
\end{itemize}
Each model works at its own resolution, so compare margins, not PSNR, between
models.}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{World action models on the seven test demonstrations, per sensor, in
  PSNR (dB, higher is better). \emph{Reconstruction}: seen frames through the
  model's own autoencoder. \emph{Prediction}: predicted future frames.
  \bl{\new{\emph{Repeat last}: the last seen frame used as the prediction for every
  future time,}} \gn{\bl{the prediction of a model that expects nothing to move.} \bl{A
  model must beat it to show that it predicts how the scene changes.}} Both averaged over the prediction horizon. \emph{Margin}:
  prediction minus repeat last, at \SI{0.8}{\second} ahead and at the end of
  each model's horizon (\SI{4.8}{\second} for DreamZero, \SI{1.1}{\second} for

  PSNR, between models.} \gn{In each row the better of prediction and repeat

\gn{\bl{\textbf{Compressing the images costs little; predicting the future costs
most.}} \bl{FastWAM's pretrained autoencoder reproduces a seen frame at 34\,dB on the
overhead camera and about 46\,dB on the fingertips, and its predictions sit 13
to 17\,dB lower, so almost all of its error is in the prediction itself.}
\bl{DreamZero's fingertip predictions (25 to 30\,dB) are likewise well below its
reconstructions (34 to 37\,dB).} \bl{The exception is DreamZero's overhead camera:
its small autoencoder reconstructs it at only 17.3\,dB, a visibly blurred image,
so its predictions (15.5\,dB) cannot be much better than they are.}

\bl{\textbf{DreamZero predicts change, but only beyond the first
\SI{0.8}{\second}.}} \bl{From \SI{1.6}{\second} ahead onwards it beats repeating the
last frame on every sensor, by roughly 3 to 8\,dB, most on the fingertips.} \bl{At
\SI{0.8}{\second} it only ties, because the scene has hardly changed yet and the
last frame is still nearly right.} \bl{Its frames are blurred, but they show the arms
leaving and the folded shorts staying, as really happened
(Figure~\ref{fig:filmstrip}).}

\bl{\textbf{FastWAM predicts sharp, correct frames, yet on the overhead camera it
only ties with repeating the last frame.}} \bl{Its predicted frames move the grippers
the right way, and their errors are confined to the moving arms and cloth
(Figure~\ref{fig:filmstrip}).} \bl{But over its one-second horizon the garment and
table, most of the image, hardly move, so the copied last frame is already
right about most pixels.} \bl{PSNR averages the error over every pixel, so a correct
prediction of a small moving part, such as a gripper, barely raises the score.}
\bl{On the fingertips, where contact changes much of the image, FastWAM beats
repeating the last frame by 3 to 6\,dB on every sensor, and at
\SI{0.8}{\second}, the one moment both models predict, it is ahead of DreamZero
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:predmargin}).}}
