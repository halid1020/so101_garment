\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We report four experiments, in the order of the questions in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Section~\ref{sec:compare} compares all the models,
Section~\ref{sec:crop} tests the tactile crop, Section~\ref{sec:sensors} asks
which sensors ACT needs \new{and which inputs each model uses}, and
Section~\ref{sec:wm} scores the world action models' predictions of the
cameras.

\textbf{Both world action models plan the next ten steps better than every
other model on unseen demonstrations.} Over those steps their test errors are
3.9 (FastWAM) and 4.2 (DreamZero). The two are too close to rank. Their lead is
several times larger than the change we see when we rerun them with different
random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). It does not prove that
predicting the future is what helps: FastWAM starts from a pretrained video
model, and DreamZero \new{is given the past \SI{1.6}{\second} of arm commands}.

  smaller than this spread is not a finding.}
  \label{tab:floor}

\rev{\textbf{Flow matching is the most accurate behaviour-cloning policy}, over
the first ten steps and further ahead: 4.3 against Diffusion's 5.0 over ten
steps, 7.3 against 7.6 over 24 and 9.0 against 9.2 over 32. Flow matching and
pi0.5 share their training objective, and flow matching's error over the first
ten steps is half pi0.5's (4.3 against 9.0): on our data, pi0.5's pretraining
with our narrow LoRA does worse than a small model trained from scratch
(Section~\ref{sec:pi05}).}

\textbf{Diffusion's error starts low but grows fastest.} On the test
demonstrations it rises from 3.9 at the first step to 13.9 by step 32, a factor
of 3.6; ACT starts at 7.3 but grows by only 1.8, so the two cross at about one
second (Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}). On the training demonstrations ACT's error is
almost flat over all 100 steps (2.4 to 3.3): it replays a familiar
demonstration almost exactly.

\textbf{Every model is much worse on unseen demonstrations.} ACT's error is
about five times higher on the test demonstrations than on the training ones,
and Diffusion's about twice as high. \new{Because the last seven demonstrations
are also the latest, a slow change over the afternoon could make them harder.
When the seven are instead picked at random, each sits between training
demonstrations in time, so drift should make them easier; the gap grows instead
(8.9 against 5.3 for ACT, 2.6 against 1.9 for Diffusion; Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:split}). Drift does not explain the gap.}

\textbf{pi0.5's small gap does not mean it generalises well.} Its error on
the demonstrations it trained on is already higher than Diffusion's on
demonstrations it never saw: after about one pass over the data, adapting only
the small LoRA addition, it never fitted the training set. \new{Trained for
about three passes (\num{70000} steps), it barely moved: training error fell
from 17.1 to 16.4 and test error from 18.4 to 18.0, less than rescoring with
another seed moves it (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). The narrow LoRA, not
too little training, is the likelier limit.}

\textbf{To every model, the test demonstrations look like training ones.} A
test demonstration is between 0.90 and 1.16 times as far from its nearest
training demonstration as a training demonstration is from its own nearest
neighbour (Table~\ref{tab:recordings}). Each test demonstration has training
demonstrations that look about as similar as training demonstrations look to
each other, so the large gaps of Section~\ref{sec:compare} are not caused by
the test demonstrations looking unfamiliar. \gr{\textbf{Split by camera, the
fingertips differ slightly more than the overhead view.} With the overhead
camera alone the ratio is 0.67 to 1.04, at or below one for every model; with
the fingertips alone it is 0.92 to 1.17, above one for ACT, flow matching,
DreamZero and FastWAM. The test demonstrations' fingertip images are a little
less like the training ones than their overhead images are, but by at most 17\,\%,
not enough to explain test errors up to seven times the training errors
(Table~\ref{tab:actions}).}

\gr{\textbf{The session does contain one visual change, at demonstration 43.} In
Figure~\ref{fig:recordings}, ACT, Diffusion and pi0.5 see the session as two
groups of demonstrations, 1 to 42 and 43 to 65: demonstrations within a group
look alike, and demonstrations from different groups look different. To find
which camera causes this, we repeated the comparison one camera at a time. Only
one camera changes, the left arm's left fingertip (LLG), and it changes all at
once between demonstrations 42 and 43; the same camera and the same boundary
appear for ACT, Diffusion, flow matching and pi0.5. Comparing that sensor's
images of the untouched gel before and after shows what happened: its view
shifted, so the gel's ridge, the edges of the pad and some marks on the gel sit
in different places (Figure~\ref{fig:sensorshift}). The gel or the camera was
probably knocked or re-seated between the two demonstrations. The change does
not separate test from training, since 15 training demonstrations (43--57) come
after it as well. DreamZero and FastWAM barely show it, probably because our
features for them average each fingertip onto about four cells of the
autoencoder's latent, too coarse to see the ridge move.}}

\rev{\subsection{Learning curves}
\label{sec:curves}

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} follows each model's training and test error over the
first ten steps through training (Section~\ref{sec:measures}). The last point
of each curve reproduces the model's row of Table~\ref{tab:actions} to within
what a rerun moves it: the curves come from fresh runs with the same settings,
and ACT's last point over the first ten steps is 8.7 against 7.9 in the table.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{curves.png}
  \caption{Action error over the first ten steps on the training (dashed) and
  test (solid) demonstrations, at every saved checkpoint of a rerun with the
  same settings. The dotted line marks the checkpoint with the lowest test
  error.}
  \label{fig:curves}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The test error stops improving long before training ends; the training
error does not.} Diffusion's test error levels off at about 5.2 by
\num{40000} of its \num{100000} steps, while its training error keeps falling,
from 3.5 to 1.8. Flow matching's levels off by \num{20000} of \num{60000}.
ACT's training error falls from 8.4 to 2.5 over the whole run, while its test
error moves only from 10.5 to about 8, and over its whole plan not at all (14.7
at \num{5000} steps, 14.3 at the end). The large gaps of
Table~\ref{tab:actions} are therefore built late in training: the extra steps
fit the training demonstrations more closely without helping on new ones.
They do not make the test error worse either, so stopping earlier would save
time, not improve the models.

\textbf{pi0.5 never pulls apart.} Its training and test errors fall together
and level off at about \num{20000} steps, with a gap of 1.1 throughout: it
stops learning rather than overfitting, as Section~\ref{sec:compare} found.

\textbf{The trainer's own held-out loss would have misled us.} LeRobot also
reports each model's training loss on the test demonstrations. For Diffusion
it rises fourfold from \num{10000} steps (0.029 to 0.119), and for flow
matching it rises too, which reads as severe overfitting, yet neither model's
test action error gets worse. These losses score how well a model removes
noise from the recorded actions, not how close its plan is to them.

\textbf{The world action models differ.} DreamZero reaches its best test error
by \num{20000} of its \num{80000} steps (3.9) and then drifts up slightly, to
4.1, while its training error falls from 1.7 to 0.8; its held-out loss rises
2.5-fold. FastWAM is the only model still improving at the end: its test error
falls from 4.6 to 4.1 to 3.9 over its \num{30000} steps, so it would probably
gain from longer training, although its held-out loss rises here too.}

\gr{\subsection{Why pi0.5 does poorly with our LoRA}
\label{sec:pi05}

pi0.5 is the one model that never fits its own training demonstrations: its
training error (17.1 over its plan; 8.3 over the first ten steps in its
learning-curve run) is higher than
every other model's \emph{test} error over those ten steps, and its learning
curve levels off by \num{20000} steps with the training and test errors moving
together (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}). That is under-fitting, not over-fitting, and
four things in how we trained it point to it.

\textbf{It saw each training frame about once.} We trained with a batch of one
(to fit a 24\,GB GPU), so \num{30000} steps show the model each of the
\num{27465} training frames 1.1 times on average. The other models see each
frame 9 (FastWAM) to 35 (flow matching) times. Training \num{70000} steps
(2.5 passes) barely moved it (Section~\ref{sec:compare}), but that is still
far fewer passes, each with a batch of one.

\textbf{It could change very little.} Our LoRA trains rank-16 corrections to
the action expert's query and value projections and to its state and action
layers: about 1.3 million weights, 0.03\,\% of the model. The vision-language
model that turns the images and the instruction into the tokens the action
expert reads stays exactly as pretrained. Published recipes adapt far more
(Appendix~\ref{sec:modeldetails}).

\textbf{Its inputs are new to it.} Two of its three image slots were pretrained
on wrist cameras; we feed them fingertip images of a gel. With the
vision-language model frozen, these reach the action expert only through
features never trained on anything like them.

\textbf{What should help}, in order of cost: a larger batch and more passes;
LoRA on the attention and feed-forward layers of both the language model and
the action expert, as in the openpi recipe; and full finetuning, which needs far
more than 24\,GB of GPU memory or sharding over several GPUs. We tested the first
two together: a rank-32 LoRA on both parts, trained with a batch of 8 for the
same \num{30000} steps (8.7 passes). \pending{wide-LoRA pi0.5 result.}}

\rev{\textbf{Cropping the tactile images changes almost nothing.} No crop but
one moves any model by more than a few per cent, in either direction
(Table~\ref{tab:crop}): within what a rerun with another random seed moves
Diffusion and pi0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). The ridge crop, which also
removes the gel's bright ridge, is no different. The one consistent cost is
ACT's: every crop that stretches the image costs it about 0.6 (14.6 to 14.7
against 14.1), while the unstretched rows-only crop costs nothing, so the
stretching, not the loss of the rim, is to blame.} \gr{The one exception is FastWAM's ridge crop, 4.21 against 3.94 uncropped
(7\,\% worse), more than rescoring with other sampler seeds moves FastWAM
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:floor}). It is a single training run, so part of it may
be what a rerun would move; its four-edge crop changed nothing.}

\textbf{The policies do not over-weight the edges of the tactile images.}
Measured with Grad-CAM on a test demonstration (Figure~\ref{fig:rim}),
the edge band of every uncropped model gets two thirds to nine tenths of its
share of the weight by area (1 would be no preference), at every band width:
\new{the strong edge weighting of Figure~\ref{fig:gradcamevidence} happens at
some moments but does not hold on average.}

crop they move to 0.45 to 0.69, where the contact marks form. pi0.5 with the
four-edge crop still peaks on the ridge (0.33 to 0.35), which is what the ridge
crop removes. Diffusion peaks just right of the ridge (0.37 to 0.53), and
DreamZero's peaks are scattered. FastWAM's map is nearly flat: painting any cell
moves its plan by at least 40\,\% as much as the most important one, and since
its pinned plan repeats exactly, this is the model, not sampling noise.}

\new{\textbf{ACT generalises better without the fingertips.} Trained on the

\subsection{How well the world action models predict the cameras}
\label{sec:wm}

Table~\ref{tab:wm} scores both world action models on every sensor. The first
column is reconstruction, the frames the model saw passed through its own
autoencoder, which is the sharpest any of its predictions can be. \new{For
DreamZero this is its frozen image autoencoder, applied to each frame; for
FastWAM it is Wan2.2's video autoencoder, which compresses groups of four
frames together.} The next columns compare its predicted future frames with the
real ones and with repeating the last frame it saw.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{World action models on the seven test demonstrations, per sensor, in
  PSNR (dB, higher is better). \emph{Reconstruction}: seen frames through the
  model's own autoencoder. \emph{Prediction}: predicted future frames.
  \new{\emph{Repeat last}: the last seen frame used as the prediction for every
  future time.} Both averaged over the prediction horizon. \emph{Margin}:
  prediction minus repeat last, at \SI{0.8}{\second} ahead and at the end of
  each model's horizon (\SI{4.8}{\second} for DreamZero, \SI{1.1}{\second} for
  FastWAM). Each model works at its own resolution, so compare margins, not
  PSNR, between models.}
  \label{tab:wm}
  \small
  \input{tables/wm_table}
\end{table}



\rev{\textbf{Most of the prediction error comes from predicting, not from
compressing the images.} FastWAM's pretrained autoencoder reproduces a seen
frame at 34\,dB on the overhead camera and about 46\,dB on the fingertips, and
its predictions sit 13 to 17\,dB below. DreamZero's fingertip predictions
(25 to 30\,dB) sit well below its autoencoder (34 to 37\,dB). The exception is
DreamZero's overhead camera, where its small autoencoder reaches only
17.3\,dB, a visibly blurred image (a typical pixel off by 14\,\% of the
brightness range), and its predictions, at 15.5\,dB, are close to that ceiling.

\textbf{DreamZero predicts the cameras better than repeating the last frame,
on every sensor, from \SI{1.6}{\second} ahead onwards,} by roughly 3 to 8\,dB,
most on the fingertips. At \SI{0.8}{\second} it only ties, because the future
still looks almost exactly like the present. Its frames are blurred, but it
predicts the arms leaving and the folded shorts staying, as really happened
(Figure~\ref{fig:filmstrip}).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{filmstrip_h2h.png}

  (test demonstration 58, 8\,s in, as the arms release the folded shorts), on

\textbf{FastWAM predicts sharp frames, but a frame-averaged score barely
rewards them.} On the overhead camera it ties with repeating the last frame,
although its frames move the grippers the right way, with the error confined
to the moving arms and cloth. The garment fills most of the frame and hardly
moves within its one-second horizon, so repeating the last frame is already
right about most pixels: \textbf{a score averaged over the whole frame can rate
a correct prediction of a small moving part as no better than doing nothing.}
On the fingertips, where contact changes many pixels, FastWAM ends 3 to 6\,dB
above repeating the last frame on every sensor, and at \SI{0.8}{\second}, the
one moment both models predict, it is ahead of DreamZero (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:predmargin}).}
